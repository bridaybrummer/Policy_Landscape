Citation Trail

Which known burden-of-disease data sources are cited in real National DoH strategic documents.

R/extract_app_citations.R downloads real National Department of Health strategic documents (Annual Performance Plan, Strategic Plans), and, since this update, 3 provincial Departments of Health’s Annual Performance Plans (Gauteng, KwaZulu-Natal, Western Cape), and searches their text for mentions of known burden-of-disease evidence sources: Stats SA, Global Burden of Disease (GBD), the SAMRC’s own National Burden of Disease Study (added 2026-09-23 — distinct from both GBD and the SAMRC’s separate NCoDV registration-validation study), SADHS, GHS, District Health Barometer, NCoDV, Rapid Mortality Surveillance, and Thembisa — plus, since a later 2026 update, mentions of NDoH’s own internal routine data systems (EDRWeb, TIER.net, DHIS, NICD), tracked as a separate source_category so they never dilute an evidence-source percentage (see “NDoH’s own routine data systems” below).

A live run across 9 confirmed documents (6 national + 3 provincial) found 188 evidence-source citation matches (plus 132 routine-data-system matches, see below) — 3 live (2020+) national documents (2020/21-2024/25 Strategic Plan, 2025/26 Annual Performance Plan, 2025-2030 Strategic Plan — only 2 distinct document years, 2020 and 2025, since two of the three are both dated 2025), 3 archived pre-2020 national Annual Performance Plans (2017/18-2019/20, 2018/19-2020/21, 2019/20-2021/22 — 3 distinct document years, one per document) found via the Internet Archive Wayback Machine (the live health.gov.za site was migrated to a new CMS and no longer serves pre-2020 documents), and 3 live provincial Annual Performance Plans found via targeted DuckDuckGo searches and individually curl-verified. The pre-2020 APPs extend coverage before COVID-19 and, critically, the 2017/18-2019/20 APP covers the exact fiscal year the NCoDV validation study’s fieldwork was conducted in. Stats SA is by far the most frequently cited source in both spheres — and provincial documents cite it even more heavily, proportionally, than national ones (see the national-vs-provincial chart below). Only 3 of 9 provinces are covered so far (see Still open).

National vs. provincial citation-source mix

Each sphere’s own total is treated as 100% here, so this compares which sources dominate citation practice in each sphere, independent of the different number of documents/pages behind each sphere’s total (see “Provincial source-mix detail” below for the full numeric breakdown and caveats — this is 6 national documents vs. only 3 of 9 provinces, not a representative provincial sample).

Note

So what: provincial documents lean on Stats SA general mentions even more heavily than national ones (71.2% vs. 55.7% of each sphere’s own total), and cite SADHS, Rapid Mortality Surveillance, or the SAMRC National Burden of Disease Study zero times across all 3 provincial documents sampled, despite meaningful national citation counts for the first two. A further real, quotable pattern: nationally, GBD and the SAMRC National Burden of Disease Study move in opposite directions across document eras — GBD’s citation share rose from 0% (pre-2020) to 12.5% (post-2020), while the SAMRC study’s share fell from 5.4% to 2.1% (see “Era comparison and phrasing variants” below). Since provinces — not the National DoH — control actual EPRE budget allocation, a citation-trail argument based only on national documents risks not being representative of the evidence base that actually informs provincial budget decisions. This 3-province sample is a first real check on that risk, not a resolution of it: the remaining 6 provinces still need to be added before treating this as conclusive.

Stats SA citations by publication theme, over time

Not all “Stats SA” mentions refer to the same underlying publication. classify_stats_sa_theme() scans each cited page’s full text for report-identifying keywords and tags every stats_sa_general/ stats_sa_cod_report match as one of: mortality (P0309 cause-of-death report), population estimates/census (mid-year population estimates, Census, Community Survey, or a provincial “Population Circular”/“Stats SA projections” denominator), vital registration (recorded births/deaths, or the “CRVS” acronym), household survey (General Household Survey), economic/labour market statistics (Quarterly Labour Force Survey, GDP, unemployment rate), socioeconomic/SDG country reporting (Stats SA’s own Sustainable Development Goals Country Report), an abbreviation/glossary-list flag (see the callout below — a real, distinct finding from a 2026 granularity audit), or general/unspecified (a bare “Stats SA” mention with no identifiable product, after all of the above are ruled out). document_year is each document’s own first fiscal year, taken directly from its title.

Note

So what: the specific Stats SA product being cited shifts across document eras — mortality (P0309 cause-of-death) citations peak in the 2020 Strategic Plan at 58.6% of that document’s Stats SA mentions (vs. 18.6-48.1% in the three earlier APPs), plausibly reflecting COVID-era attention to mortality surveillance, while population estimates/census citations dominate the 2017 APP (72.1%) and the 2024 Gauteng APP (63.3%). The most recent APPs (2025: national, KZN, Western Cape) had originally looked like the most vaguely sourced year (39.4% classified “general/unspecified” — see the earlier version of this analysis in docs/KNOWLEDGE.md) — but that figure did not survive a closer, 2026 granularity audit of every page it came from (see next callout): once genuine products hiding in that bucket were identified, 2025’s true unresolved “general/unspecified” share fell to 12.7%, only modestly above 2020’s 20.7% and well below 2017-2019’s range. A citation that still doesn’t say which Stats SA publication it relies on cannot be checked against that publication’s own known data-quality caveats (e.g. mid-year population estimates being modelled projections, or P0309 cause-of-death data inheriting the ill-defined/garbage-code problem this project’s KNOWLEDGE.md documents) — vague sourcing remains a small, real symptom of the same data-quality-ownership gap the abstract’s second recommendation addresses, even after this more careful accounting shrinks its apparent size.

Important

Granularity audit finding (2026): a meaningful share of “Stats SA” matches are not citations at all. Reading the real page text behind every “general/unspecified” match revealed that 10 of the 58 combined national+provincial stats_sa_general/stats_sa_cod_report matches (17%) are not evidence citations — they are front-matter abbreviation/glossary-list entries that happen to define “Stats SA” as an acronym (e.g. “…SRH Sexual and Reproductive Health StatsSA Statistics South Africa STG Standard Treatment Guidelines…”), found on 5 distinct glossary pages across 5 documents. These are now tagged abbreviation/glossary list (not a substantive citation) rather than folded into “general/unspecified”, and should be excluded from any count of substantive evidence citations. Nationally, this means 6 of the headline 68 “Stats SA (general)” matches quoted in the abstract are glossary noise, not citations — the raw 68/122 count is still literally accurate (these are real regex matches), but a stricter “was a specific, checkable Stats SA product actually named or implied” count would be lower. The remaining, genuinely reviewed “general/unspecified” mentions were mostly one-off, hard-to-classify fragments (e.g. “StatsSA is General Total number…”, a mangled PDF-table cell; or “Stats Sa Legislature presentation, 2021”, citing gender-based-violence statistics via a parliamentary presentation rather than a named Stats SA product directly) — genuinely ambiguous secondary sourcing, not a coding gap. Several other real, specific products were previously miscounted as “unspecified” purely because the classifier’s keyword list was too narrow: the General Household Survey (5 matches, e.g. “Stats SA General Household Survey, 2016”), Quarterly Labour Force Survey/economic indicators (3 matches, e.g. GDP growth, unemployment rate), Stats SA’s own Sustainable Development Goals Country Report (4 matches), population-projection-based denominators cited as “Population Circular”/“Stats SA projections” (3 matches, Western Cape indicator definitions), the “CRVS” acronym for civil registration and vital statistics (2 matches), and a plural “causes of death” phrasing that the original singular-only regex had silently missed (found in the KZN APP, now correctly tagged mortality). None of this changes the total citation count (188 rows, 122 national + 66 provincial, unchanged) — only the theme label attached to existing stats_sa_general/ stats_sa_cod_report rows.

Note

Thembisa: a single, provincial-only mention. Thembisa — South Africa’s own domestically-produced HIV/AIDS epidemiological model (used by SANAC, National Treasury, and UNAIDS for the country’s official HIV/TB estimates) — is cited exactly once across all 9 sampled strategic documents, and only in the Western Cape’s 2025/26 APP: “the Thembisa 4.5 model shows that the population of older people living with HIV has increased (and will continue…” (used to support a TB loss-to-follow-up monitoring point, not as a primary evidence citation for HIV burden estimates). It does not appear at all in any of the 6 national documents sampled, nor in Gauteng’s or KZN’s APPs. Given Thembisa’s central role in South Africa’s own official HIV/TB estimates, its near-total absence from the strategic documents that justify these same programmes’ budgets is a further, specific illustration of the citation-sourcing gap this chapter documents — though n=1 is too small a sample to generalise beyond “this specific set of 9 documents does not lean on Thembisa as a named evidence source.”

Stats SA (general) dominance is stable across both eras — not a recent or COVID-era artefact. SADHS and the SAMRC’s own National Burden of Disease Study both skew toward the older documents, while GBD is the only source moving the other way (0% pre-2020 → 12.5% post-2020).

Phrasing: the no-space “StatsSA” form is the single most common literal match nationally (39 of 68 stats_sa_general matches, 57%), but provincial documents skew toward the full “Statistics South Africa” (26 of 47 provincial matches, 55%) — a reminder that a regex tuned on national documents can still miscount if applied uncritically to a different sphere’s house style (it happened to catch both variants correctly here).

Registered mortality vs. corrected-burden vs. research/validation, over time

The comparisons above treat every “Stats SA”, “GBD”, etc. mention as its own separate bucket. This section instead asks the CPHIA abstract’s actual question directly: across the same national documents, how does citing registered mortality data (Stats SA’s mortality/P0309 cause-of-death reporting) compare, over time, to citing either a corrected-burden estimate (GBD, the SAMRC’s own National Burden of Disease Study) or a research/validation source that could be used to check registered data’s accuracy (NCoDV, Rapid Mortality Surveillance, Thembisa)? classify_source_group() (R/extract_app_citations.R) buckets every citation into exactly one of these three groups or “other” (population estimates, vital registration, SADHS, GHS, District Health Barometer, and NDoH’s own routine data systems are deliberately left in “other” here — not dissected further in this comparison).

Per-group share of each era’s own national total (same 6 national documents — 3 pre-2020, 3 post-2020, 74 vs. 48 citations, see above), for a direct year-over-year comparison:

Tracked group Pre-2020 n Pre-2020 % Post-2020 n Post-2020 %
Registered mortality (Stats SA) 18 24.3% 13 27.1%
Corrected-burden estimate (GBD + SAMRC NBOD) 4 5.4% 7 14.6%
Research/validation (NCoDV + RMS + Thembisa) 6 8.1% 4 8.3%
Other (not yet categorised) 46 62.2% 24 50.0%
Total 74 100% 48 100%
Note

Why “Registered mortality” here (18/13) is smaller than “Stats SA (general)” + “Stats SA cause-of-death report” combined in the source-level table above (39+7=46 pre-2020, 29+2=31 post-2020): classify_source_group() only assigns a stats_sa_general match to registered_mortality when classify_stats_sa_theme() has tagged that specific page match as mortality/P0309-cause-of-death themed; stats_sa_cod_report matches always count in full. Most stats_sa_general matches are themed as population estimates, household survey, general/unspecified, or other non-mortality Stats SA content instead, and those fall into “Other” here even though they’re counted under the broader “Stats SA (general)” source in the table above. Concretely: of the 46 combined pre-2020 stats_sa_general/stats_sa_cod_report matches, only 18 are mortality-themed (13 of 31 post-2020) — the rest are folded into “Other” in this stricter, group-level comparison.

Note

So what: registered mortality reporting is not being displaced by more corrected-burden or validation engagement over time — its own citation share rose slightly (24.3% → 27.1%). What actually changed is that corrected-burden citations nearly tripled in share (5.4% → 14.6%, driven almost entirely by GBD, per the era table above), while research/validation sources — the sources that could actually justify trusting registered data, or correcting it — stayed essentially flat (8.1% → 8.3%). And that 8.1-8.3% is carried almost entirely by Rapid Mortality Surveillance: NCoDV — the source of this project’s own 36.9% validation estimate — has zero confirmed citations across all 9 sampled documents, and Thembisa is cited exactly once, provincially only (see the callouts above). Registered mortality data is being read alongside a growing amount of corrected-burden evidence, but not alongside a growing amount of the validation research that would explain why registered and corrected estimates disagree in the first place.

NDoH’s own routine data systems

Distinct from the burden-of-disease evidence sources above (Stats SA, GBD, SADHS, etc.), these documents also cite the routine M&E/patient- monitoring data systems NDoH itself operates and reports through: EDRWeb, TIER.net, DHIS, and NICD. Added 2026 in response to the question “what other data sources feed these documents, beyond national statistics agencies?” — tracked as a separate source_category (routine_data_system) throughout so it never dilutes any of the evidence-source percentages quoted above or in the abstract.

Note

So what: these routine data systems are cited 132 times across all 9 documents (58 national + 74 provincial) — a real, substantial citation volume in its own right, comparable to the entire evidence-source total (122 national). DHIS (the District Health Information System) is the single most-cited routine system in both spheres (31 national, 50 provincial), followed by TIER.net (South Africa’s ART/TB patient-monitoring register, 9 national, 16 provincial). NICD citations skew national (14 national vs. 1 provincial) — plausibly because NICD’s mandate is national communicable-disease surveillance, not routine provincial service delivery. This is a distinct citation-sourcing story from the evidence-source analysis above: it says which data infrastructure these plans report performance through, not which epidemiological evidence justifies their targets — both matter for a full data-quality-ownership picture, but they should not be added together into one blended percentage.

Still open

Provincial extension: partially done. 3 of 9 provinces (Gauteng, KwaZulu-Natal, Western Cape — the 3 largest by population and Health budget share) are now in the registry, confirmed via targeted DuckDuckGo searches and individually curl-verified before being added (never guessed). The remaining 6 provinces’ APP/Strategic Plan URLs are not yet confirmed. Matches are still whole-source mentions, not yet tied to a specific cited report year or budget line. Regex matching counts textual mentions, not verified data usage — a document could name a source once and still base its actual targets on a different, unmatched source. Raw per-document counts are also not length-normalized (the longest document, ndoh_app_2017_18_2019_20, has more total pages, not unusually dense citation per page).

Pre-2020 extension: done. The document registry previously started at the 2020/21-2024/25 Strategic Plan; it now also includes 3 real archived Annual Performance Plans (2017/18-2019/20, 2018/19-2020/21, 2019/20-2021/22), each confirmed via the Wayback Machine and verified by downloading and checking the actual title page before being registered. This grew the citation-event sample from 47 to 122 national matches (188 total including provincial, after also adding the SAMRC National Burden of Disease Study pattern on 2026-09-23) and, for the first time, gives citation-trail coverage of the exact 2017/18 fiscal year the NCoDV validation study’s fieldwork was conducted in. Two candidate documents were investigated and ruled out as out of scope (a disease-specific Malaria Elimination Strategic Plan, and SANAC’s HIV/TB/STI National Strategic Plan — a different organisation, not NDoH); two further candidates had corrupted/truncated Wayback captures and were unusable. See docs/KNOWLEDGE.md for full detail. This citation-mix evidence (how heavily registered vs. burden-corrected sources are cited, and whether that holds at the provincial level where budgets are actually set) remains the project’s strongest starting point for motivating a CRVS data-quality assessment.
