Population vs. budget allocation

Does provincial Health budget allocation track population size? This compares each province’s FY2024 Main appropriation budget share against its real Census 2022 population share (SA_PROVINCE_POPULATION_CENSUS2022, see docs/KNOWLEDGE.md), computing an allocation_ratio = budget_share / population_share (>1 = over-allocated relative to population share, <1 = under-allocated).

Note

So what: on this population-size-only check, provincial Health budget shares track population shares fairly closely (allocation ratios mostly within ±14% of 1.0) — a materially different picture from the raw-rand ranking, where Gauteng and KZN’s combined 45.7% share looks disproportionate until population is accounted for (together they hold ~44% of the national population). Mpumalanga (0.87) and Limpopo (0.90) sit furthest below 1.0 and are the most plausible candidates for genuine population-adjusted under-allocation — but this uses total population, not a health-system- specific denominator (e.g. medical-scheme coverage), and no adjustment for age structure, disease burden, or rurality/cost-of-delivery, so treat this as a first screening check, not a resource-allocation-formula critique. Census 2022 is also a single point-in-time count (10 October 2022), not a mid-year estimate matched to FY2024 — treat allocation_ratio as approximate, not exact.

So what: ~93% of the Health budget is “Current payments” (staff/goods & services) in both FY2023 and FY2024, leaving under 5% as a capital envelope — any new infrastructure ask (including CRVS-adjacent data-systems asks) competes for a small, largely pre-committed slice.

Programme composition

Note

So what: District Health Services is both the single largest programme (~47%) and the one closest to facility-level mortality/notification data — the natural home for a CRVS-data-quality argument aimed at provincial Health budgets.

HIV/AIDS + TB subprogramme base rate

Note

So what: HIV/AIDS + TB subprogrammes account for ~10.6-10.8% of the provincial Health budget (R27.4bn in FY2024) — a real base rate for the excluded population this thesis deliberately doesn’t analyse (see the Plan & Open Questions chapter’s “Future / related projects” section). It is entirely domestic government money as far as this dataset can see — EPRE does not capture foreign donor financing (PEPFAR, Global Fund) at all.

Overall budget size and growth

Total Main appropriation, all 9 provinces: R242.89bn (FY2023) → R259.25bn (FY2024) — nominal growth of +6.74%.

Note

So what: this is a nominal figure only — no CPI adjustment has been applied. Any claim that “health budgets are/aren’t keeping pace” must state whether it means nominal or real terms.

Provincial distribution

Gauteng and KwaZulu-Natal together account for 45.7% of the combined 9-province Health budget (FY2023+FY2024 combined) — roughly 5x the smallest province (Northern Cape, 2.5%).

Note

So what: any downstream CRVS-data-quality finding expressed in absolute rand will be dominated by Gauteng and KZN purely because of their size. Report per-capita or share-of-budget figures alongside absolute rand so a real but proportionally large issue in a smaller province (e.g. Northern Cape, Free State) isn’t visually dwarfed in a combined chart.

Funded by the local fiscus or foreign aid?

A likely CPHIA audience question, answered honestly from what this dataset can actually show:

  • Every rand above is domestic South African government money by construction — EPRE (Estimates of Provincial Revenue and Expenditure) is a Treasury/provincial-legislature budget document, not a donor ledger.
  • Not yet split into a province’s own discretionary (“equitable share”) money vs. a national conditional grant (e.g. the HIV, TB, Malaria and Community Outreach Grant) — that split lives in the Division of Revenue Act annexures, a different Treasury publication not yet pulled.
  • Foreign donor financing (PEPFAR, Global Fund) is structurally outside this dataset, not merely small or unmeasured within it — PEPFAR historically runs substantially off-budget via implementing partners.
Note

So what: the ~10.6-10.8% domestic-only HIV/TB share above is a floor, not a ceiling. Don’t imply either “it’s all domestic” or “we can’t rule out donor dependency” — both overstate certainty. Quantifying the donor-vs-domestic mix is the explicit next step scoped in the Plan & Open Questions chapter’s Thembisa/HIV-TB future project.

Budget credibility (planned vs. actual spend): not yet computable

Comparing Main appropriation (tabled budget) against Audited Outcome (final audited actual spend) for the same year would be a real, independent signal of planning/execution quality — but in this EPRE extract, no financial year has both phases present (Audited Outcome only exists for FY2020-FY2022; Main appropriation only for FY2023-FY2024).

Note

So what: we cannot currently say whether provinces over- or under-spend relative to plan. This is an open action item (pull an additional EPRE release/vintage, or use Treasury’s in-year monitoring publications), not a fabricated null result.

Warning

These figures are the BUDGET (implied allocation) side only — not a funding gap. A true funding gap would also need a burden-corrected mortality/morbidity comparison, and that side remains blocked (Stats SA, NCoDV, GBD — see the Plan & Open Questions chapter). Do not read the figures below as “the funding gap.”

Real National Treasury Estimates of National Expenditure (ENE) data, Vote 18: Health, 2025-26 release — the national counterpart to the provincial EPRE data above. Unlike EPRE, the national ENE has a genuine disease-specific programme: “Communicable and Non-communicable Diseases” (CNCD).

CNCD’s share of the whole Vote 18: Health budget

Note

So what: the CNCD programme is roughly two-fifths of the entire national Health vote, not a minor line item — how this ~40% is divided internally (below) has first-order consequences for the whole department’s budget.

CNCD subprogramme composition over time

Note

So what: HIV/AIDS+STIs’ share of the CNCD programme rose from ~76% (2021/22) to ~99% (2024/25 onward) — a Treasury/NDoH programme-design trend a budget analyst can act on directly, independent of any burden-of-disease evidence.

Genuinely non-HIV/TB/MCWH disease spending within CNCD

Note

So what: by 2024/25, genuinely non-HIV/TB/MCWH disease spending (Communicable Diseases + Non-communicable Diseases + Health Promotion and Nutrition combined) is ~0.70% of the CNCD programme — roughly 0.28% of the entire Vote 18: Health budget. The 2021/22-2022/23 figures are far higher and plausibly COVID-19-inflated (not independently confirmed). This is a stark, directly actionable number for anyone arguing NCD programming is underfunded relative to its likely share of deaths — even before the burden-corrected comparison becomes possible.

Completing this into a true funding gap

This is the BUDGET (allocation) side only. Turning it into an actual funding-gap computation needs: a registered (Stats SA) cause-of-death breakdown for the same disease categories; NCoDV-style correction factors; a GBD cross-check; and a decision on the unit of comparison (rand vs. share-of-budget vs. per-capita). All four are currently blocked — see the Plan & Open Questions chapter. Until then, cite these figures as “share of the CNCD allocation,” never as “the funding gap.”

A live extraction across 6 national strategic documents found 122 citations of known burden-of-disease/vital-statistics sources. So what: the headline abstract finding lives in this table. Stats SA’s dominance is stable across both document eras (52.7% pre-2020 vs. 60.4% post-2020) — not a recent artefact. The two corrected-burden sources move in opposite directions: GBD’s share rose from 0% to 12.5%, while the SAMRC’s own National Burden of Disease Study’s share fell from 5.4% to 2.1% — engagement with South Africa’s own domestically-produced corrected-burden estimate is shrinking even as engagement with a foreign one grows. Extending to the 3 sampled provincial documents (Gauteng 39, KwaZulu-Natal 14, Western Cape 13 citations) finds Stats SA cited even more heavily proportionally (71.2% of each sphere’s own total vs. 55.7% nationally), and the SAMRC National Burden of Disease Study cited zero times across all 3 provincial documents — the sphere of government that actually sets EPRE allocations shows the least engagement with the domestic corrected-burden estimate.

Framing options for the abstract

Tip

This chapter is the ingredients pantry, not a locked-in argument. Every table, chart, and “so what” above is a raw fact this project can draw on. The titles and questions below are draft framing options for turning some subset of these ingredients into the CPHIA abstract (or a future paper) — none are decided; treat this as a menu to choose from (and combine), not a commitment.

Alternative titles to consider

Current working title (06-cphia-abstract.qmd): “Are African health budgets calibrated to health data quality? A pipeline for auditing civil registration and vital statistics (CRVS) data quality against provincial health budget allocation in South Africa” — the CRVS/data-quality framing. Alternatives grounded in specific findings above:

  • “Does population size predict health budget allocation? A province-level check against South Africa’s 2022 Census” — narrower, population-vs-budget-ratio-only framing (see Population vs. budget allocation above); easiest to defend since it needs no burden-of-disease data at all, but says nothing about disease-specific need.
  • “When one disease colonises a programme: HIV/AIDS’s rising share of South Africa’s national Communicable and Non-communicable Diseases budget, 2021-2025” — built on the CNCD subprogramme-composition trend (~76% to ~99%, see CNCD subprogramme composition over time above); a sharper, more provocative framing but narrower in scope than the CRVS argument.
  • “The vanishing 0.3%: what’s left for non-HIV/TB/maternal-child disease programming in South Africa’s national health budget” — built on the non-HIV/TB/MCWH ~0.28%-of-Vote-18 finding (see Genuinely non-HIV/TB/MCWH disease spending within CNCD above); punchy and concrete, but a single-number headline risks inviting “is that actually a problem?” pushback without the burden-of-disease comparison this project doesn’t yet have.
  • “Budgeted for what we can count: how South Africa’s health planning documents cite (and don’t cite) burden-of-disease evidence” — citation-trail-led framing (see the Citation Trail chapter); leads with the evidence-use finding rather than the budget-allocation finding, positioning this as a data-quality/evidence-use paper first and a budget paper second.
  • “What would it take to know if South Africa’s health budget is enough? A descriptive audit of data, budget, and the missing burden-of-disease comparison” — the most honest-about-limitations option; frames the whole project as scoping/diagnostic work rather than a completed funding-gap finding, which may suit a conceptual/ introductory abstract better than a sharper, single-number title would.

Varying questions to consider

Different research-question framings this same dataset can support — useful for deciding which “so what” to lead with in the abstract’s Results/Conclusion, independent of the title chosen above:

  1. Allocation-vs-population: does provincial Health budget allocation track population size, and which provinces sit furthest from parity? (Answerable now, no burden-of-disease data needed — see Population vs. budget allocation above.)
  2. Allocation-vs-burden (the CRVS framing): is health budget allocation calibrated to burden-of-disease evidence, or only to registered/official statistics? (Currently blocked — needs the Stats SA/NCoDV/GBD funding-gap comparison, see the Plan & Open Questions chapter.)
  3. Programme crowding-out: has HIV/AIDS+STIs’ rising share of the CNCD programme actually crowded out other communicable/ non-communicable disease spending over time, or has the CNCD programme simply grown enough that other subprogrammes’ absolute rand hasn’t shrunk even as their share has? (Answerable now from ENE data; the current chapter only shows share, not the absolute-rand trend for other subprogrammes — a real, addressable gap.)
  4. Evidence-use, not allocation: independent of whether the budget is “right,” which data sources do South Africa’s own planning documents actually cite as evidence — and is that mix shifting away from specific, checkable products toward vaguer, harder-to-audit citations over time? (See the Citation Trail chapter’s Stats SA publication-theme-over-time finding.)
  5. Domestic vs. donor framing: how much of South Africa’s HIV/TB response is even visible in domestic budget data (EPRE/ENE) at all, given PEPFAR/Global Fund financing sits structurally outside it? (See Funded by the local fiscus or foreign aid? above — currently only answerable as a floor, not an exact split.)
  6. Planning-vs-execution: independent of whether the planned budget is calibrated to anything, does the province actually spend what it budgets? (Currently unanswerable — see Budget credibility above; would need an additional EPRE vintage with overlapping Main-appropriation and Audited-Outcome years.)

None of these are mutually exclusive — the current abstract draft already leans on a version of (2) and (4) together. Picking a primary question should probably come before finalising the title, not after.

Methodology, limitations & traceability

Provincial (EPRE): totals use Main appropriation only (FY2023-FY2024); programme names are harmonised via apply_programme_crosswalk() (whitespace/spelling normalisation only — see data/crosswalks/programme_name_variants_2024_25.csv); the HIV/AIDS+TB figures are a text-match on subprogramme labels, not an audited HIV/TB programme classification, so a differently worded subprogramme could be missed. The population-adjusted allocation ratio uses real Census 2022 province population shares (SA_PROVINCE_POPULATION_CENSUS2022, R/constants.R) against total population only — no age-structure, disease-burden, or medical-scheme-coverage adjustment, so it is a first screening check, not a resource-allocation-formula critique. Source: fetch_vulekamali() (R/fetch_vulekamali.R); summary functions in R/describe_budget_lines.R (incl. summarise_population_adjusted_allocation()); runner Rscript scripts/run_budget_descriptive_stats.R (writes data/processed/budget_population_comparison.csv among others); tests in tests/testthat/test-describe_budget_lines.R.

National (ENE): the 2021/22-2022/23 “Communicable Diseases” spike is a plausible but not independently confirmed COVID-19 booking effect. Only 2024/25-2027/28 are available for the CNCD-share-of-vote comparison (the vote-wide total sheet only covers those years). Source: fetch_national_ene_health() (R/fetch_national_ene.R); analysis in R/analyse_disease_programme_allocation.R; runner Rscript scripts/run_national_disease_allocation.R; tests in tests/testthat/test-fetch_national_ene.R and tests/testthat/test-analyse_disease_programme_allocation.R.

All CSVs behind this chapter are real and re-derivable from the scripts above — nothing here is estimated or invented.
