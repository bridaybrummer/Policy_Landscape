Title

What Evidence Is Cited in Health Policy Documents? A Cross-Country Citation Landscape Across Eight Countries in the Bloomberg Philanthropies Data for Health (D4H) Initiative

Abstract

Background. Evidence cited in health policy and budget documents is measurable, but citation does not establish influence on decisions. Mapping these references can describe the documented evidence base without assuming that citation frequency reflects policy use.

Objectives. To describe, across South Africa, Zambia, Rwanda, Thailand, Morocco, Uganda, India, and Kenya, which categories of population-health evidence are cited in sampled health strategic plans, policies, annual performance and statistical reports, and health or national budget documents.

Methods. Targeted searches identified 52 publicly available documents (2011–2025): South Africa (9), India (11), Morocco (7), Rwanda (6), Uganda (6), Kenya (5), Thailand (4), Zambia (4). The sample comprised 49 national documents and 3 South African provincial plans. Country-scoped pattern matching classified extracted text into evidence categories, including routine health-information systems (e.g. DHIS, KHIS, HMIS). A matched record was one distinct matched wording for a source on a document page, not an independent citation or unique reference. Population estimates were classified by producer: locally produced (national statistics offices, censuses, national surveys) or internationally produced (DHS Program, PHIA, UN, World Bank, WHO, UNICEF). Both were reported as a share of all evidence records, then excluded from the denominator for other evidence categories. Routine health-information systems were counted as an evidence category. Mentions of implementing agencies (Rwanda Biomedical Centre, KEMRI) were not.

Results. The documents yielded 1,994 matched records: 1,922 were classified as evidence. Population estimates, pooled, were the largest evidence group in 6 of 8 countries (routine systems led in South Africa and Uganda; 990/1,922, 52%). Locally produced estimates accounted for 615 records (62% of population estimates) and internationally produced estimates for 375; local estimates outnumbered international ones in India, Morocco, Thailand and South Africa, and international estimates predominated in Kenya, Rwanda, Uganda and Zambia. Excluding population estimates left 932 evidence records. Routine health-information systems were the most-cited category among these (404/932, 43.3%; 6 of 8 countries, none detected in Morocco or Thailand). National vital statistics were detected in 6 of 8 countries (291/932, 31.2%), with none detected in Uganda or Morocco. GBD appeared in 5 countries (25/932, 2.7%): South Africa (11), Kenya (11), Rwanda (1), Uganda (1), and Zambia (1). Only South Africa had matched domestic burden-of-disease estimates (5/932, 0.5%). Named local epidemiological models appeared in 3 countries (South Africa, Zambia, Uganda; 7/932, 0.8%). Verbal autopsy appeared in 4 countries (1–7 records each; 12/932, 1.3%). Local disease-surveillance systems appeared in 5 countries (107/932, 11.5%). HDSS infrastructure was detected only in Kenya (1/932, 0.1%).

Conclusion. Population estimates dominated matched evidence records, with local and international producers varying by country; routine health-information systems and vital statistics predominated among the remaining evidence categories. Burden estimates, verbal autopsy, models, and HDSS were detected unevenly. These findings describe the sampled documents and current pattern coverage, not a hierarchy of evidence use in decision-making. Unequal document counts, lengths, and genres limit country comparisons. Contextual validation and linkage to budget decisions are needed before inferring investment priorities or effects on spending efficiency.

Keywords

Evidence-informed policy; health financing; civil registration and vital statistics (CRVS); burden of disease; comparative health systems; low- and middle-income countries; health information systems.

Challenging views (critical appraisal, not part of the submission)

A critical reading of this abstract’s own logic, included for honesty rather than resolved:

  1. Citation is not use. A document citing a data source does not establish that the source informed the actual decision, and a source’s absence from a planning document does not establish that it was ignored — it may simply not be the kind of document where that evidence is normally cited. The central premise, that counting citations reveals “what evidence informs policy,” conflates textual reference with decision influence.
  2. Heterogeneous document types inflate apparent differences. Pooling raw counts across an 11-page policy brief, an 1,143-page budget book, and a 650-page statistical profile treats units of very different length and genre as comparable observations; a country’s citation share may track document volume and type mix more than any underlying difference in evidence culture.
  3. Sample depth varies nearly three-fold across countries (4 documents for Thailand/Zambia vs. 11 for India), so cross-country percentage comparisons carry very different amounts of noise per country — a genuinely common citation type can look categorically “absent” in a thin sample purely by chance.
  4. The method cannot detect what it wasn’t built to look for. Pattern coverage was built inductively, country by country, from the documents at hand; a true citation phrased in wording the patterns don’t anticipate is invisible to this pipeline. “Zero citations” is reported identically whether the evidence is genuinely absent or merely phrased outside the known pattern set — the Results cannot distinguish these two cases.
  5. The taxonomy was derived from the same corpus it classifies. Evidence categories were defined inductively from what these documents actually contained, then used to describe what these documents contain — a degree of circularity worth naming rather than presenting as an independently validated instrument.
  6. The recommendation outruns the evidence. A low citation count for a category (e.g. HDSS, domestic burden estimates) is consistent with at least three different explanations — genuine absence of that evidence product in-country, its presence but citation elsewhere (academic literature, not planning documents), or a pattern-coverage gap in this study — and this abstract cannot adjudicate between them, yet the Conclusion still recommends targeting investment as if absence were the only explanation.
  7. The proposed next step assumes its own premise. Testing whether citation practice predicts budget apportionment presupposes that citation practice is diagnostically relevant to budget decisions at all; apportionment may instead follow political and historical path-dependency largely independent of what evidence a planning document cites, in which case a null result there would say nothing about data quality — only about this one causal pathway.

Findings are provisional until demonstrated - see the Plan & Open Questions chapter.
