Title

What Evidence Informs National Health Policy? A Cross-Country Citation Landscape Across Eight Countries in the Bloomberg Philanthropies Data for Health (D4H) Initiative

Abstract

Background. We mapped which categories of population-health evidence — census and survey data, civil registration and vital statistics, demographic surveillance, verbal autopsy, global and domestic burden-of-disease estimates, country-specific epidemiological models, and local disease-surveillance systems — are cited in the national health planning and budget documents of eight countries in the Bloomberg Philanthropies Data for Health (D4H) Initiative, using one consistent, auditable classification method.

Objectives. To describe, across South Africa, Zambia, Rwanda, Thailand, Morocco, Uganda, India, and Kenya, which categories of population-health evidence are cited in each country’s national health strategic plans, policies, annual performance and statistical reports, and health or national budget documents.

Methods. Publicly available national-level documents (2011-2025) were identified by targeted search and individually verified (HTTP status, content type, title-page confirmation). 52 documents were registered: South Africa (9, including 3 provincial Annual Performance Plans), India (11), Morocco (7), Rwanda (6), Uganda (6), Kenya (5), Thailand (4), and Zambia (4). Citations were extracted programmatically and classified into a unified evidence-category taxonomy, with country-scoped pattern matching to prevent acronym collisions across countries (e.g. “DHS” denotes a district health system in Thailand but a Demographic and Health Survey elsewhere). Each country’s own routine health-information system (e.g. DHIS, KHIS, HMIS) was tracked separately from evidence citations throughout.

Results. The 52 documents yielded 1,966 citation matches: 1,490 to evidence sources and 476 to routine health-information systems. Population-based estimates were the single most-cited evidence category in every country (962/1,490, 65%). National vital statistics were cited in 6 of 8 countries (291 citations), absent from Uganda and Morocco. The Global Burden of Disease study was cited in 5 of 8 countries (South Africa and Kenya, 11 citations each; Rwanda, Uganda, and Zambia, 1 each), absent from Thailand, Morocco, and India. Only South Africa cited a domestically-produced burden-of-disease estimate distinct from the global study. A named local epidemiological model appeared in 3 of 8 countries, each for that country’s own HIV response (South Africa, Zambia, Uganda). Verbal autopsy was cited in 4 of 8 countries (1-7 citations each). A named local disease-surveillance system (e.g. Integrated Disease Surveillance and Response) was cited in 5 of 8 countries (107 citations) — a citation type with no equivalent in prior burden-of-disease citation frameworks. HDSS infrastructure was cited in only 1 of 8 countries (Kenya), despite verified HDSS research sites in several sampled countries.

Conclusion. Population-based survey and census data dominate the evidence cited in documents that frame national health policy and budgets across all 8 countries, while global burden-of-disease estimates, domestic corrected-burden studies, verbal autopsy, and HDSS infrastructure are cited unevenly and, in most countries, rarely or not at all. This citation landscape identifies where to target investment in strengthening evidence systems — correcting registered data, building domestic burden-of-disease estimates, extending demographic surveillance and verbal autopsy coverage — in each country. The next step is to establish how far these cited evidence sources actually influence the proportioning of finite health budgets, and whether known data-quality gaps are reflected in how funds are apportioned across programmes.

Keywords

Evidence-informed policy; health financing; civil registration and vital statistics (CRVS); burden of disease; comparative health systems; low- and middle-income countries; health information systems.

Challenging views (critical appraisal, not part of the submission)

A critical reading of this abstract’s own logic, included for honesty rather than resolved:

  1. Citation is not use. A document citing a data source does not establish that the source informed the actual decision, and a source’s absence from a planning document does not establish that it was ignored — it may simply not be the kind of document where that evidence is normally cited. The central premise, that counting citations reveals “what evidence informs policy,” conflates textual reference with decision influence.
  2. Heterogeneous document types inflate apparent differences. Pooling raw counts across an 11-page policy brief, an 1,143-page budget book, and a 650-page statistical profile treats units of very different length and genre as comparable observations; a country’s citation share may track document volume and type mix more than any underlying difference in evidence culture.
  3. Sample depth varies nearly three-fold across countries (4 documents for Thailand/Zambia vs. 11 for India), so cross-country percentage comparisons carry very different amounts of noise per country — a genuinely common citation type can look categorically “absent” in a thin sample purely by chance.
  4. The method cannot detect what it wasn’t built to look for. Pattern coverage was built inductively, country by country, from the documents at hand; a true citation phrased in wording the patterns don’t anticipate is invisible to this pipeline. “Zero citations” is reported identically whether the evidence is genuinely absent or merely phrased outside the known pattern set — the Results cannot distinguish these two cases.
  5. The taxonomy was derived from the same corpus it classifies. Evidence categories were defined inductively from what these documents actually contained, then used to describe what these documents contain — a degree of circularity worth naming rather than presenting as an independently validated instrument.
  6. The recommendation outruns the evidence. A low citation count for a category (e.g. HDSS, domestic burden estimates) is consistent with at least three different explanations — genuine absence of that evidence product in-country, its presence but citation elsewhere (academic literature, not planning documents), or a pattern-coverage gap in this study — and this abstract cannot adjudicate between them, yet the Conclusion still recommends targeting investment as if absence were the only explanation.
  7. The proposed next step assumes its own premise. Testing whether citation practice predicts budget apportionment presupposes that citation practice is diagnostically relevant to budget decisions at all; apportionment may instead follow political and historical path-dependency largely independent of what evidence a planning document cites, in which case a null result there would say nothing about data quality — only about this one causal pathway.

Findings are provisional until demonstrated - see the Plan & Open Questions chapter.
