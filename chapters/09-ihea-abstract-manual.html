Title

What Evidence is Found in National Health Policy? A Cross-Country Citation Landscape Analysis of Eight Countries in the Data for Health (D4H) Initiative

Suggested track

Brian Brummer - D4H Initiative …

Abstract

Background. Multiple factors influence the decisions made for health strategy and budgets. A measurable factor is the evidence cited in national strategic plans, policies and budget documents. This study aims to systematically map and analyze these citations across multiple countries to describe the evidence informing health policy and potential apportioning of finances responsive to the local disease burden.

Objectives. To describe, across South Africa, Zambia, Rwanda, Thailand, Morocco, Uganda, India, and Kenya, which categories of evidence are cited in each country’s national health strategic plans, policies, and budget documents.

Methods. Claude Sonnet 5.5 model through Copilot was used to create python scripts for document scraping and extraction. Publicly available national-level documents (2011-2025) were identified by targeted search and individually verified (HTTP status, content type, title-page confirmation). 52 documents were registered: South Africa (9, including 3 provincial Annual Performance Plans), India (11), Morocco (7), Rwanda (6), Uganda (6), Kenya (5), Thailand (4), and Zambia (4). Citations were extracted and classified into an evidence-category taxonomy, with country-scoped pattern matching to prevent acronym collisions across countries (e.g. “DHS” denotes a district health system in Thailand but a Demographic and Health Survey elsewhere). Each country’s own routine health-information system (e.g. DHIS, KHIS, HMIS) was counted as an evidence category; mentions of implementing agencies (e.g. Rwanda Biomedical Centre, KEMRI) were not. Population estimates were classified by producer: locally produced (national statistics offices, censuses, national surveys) or internationally produced (DHS Program, PHIA, UN/World Bank/WHO/UNICEF). We describe the proportion of population-estimate citations and then exclude them when describing other evidence categories, as population estimates, other than population structure, do not reveal disease burden.

Results. The 52 documents yielded 1,994 citation matches: 1,922 to evidence sources. Overall, population-based estimates were the most-cited evidence group (990/1,922, 52%): 615 (62%) locally produced and 375 (38%) internationally produced. Local estimates predominated in India, Morocco, Thailand and South Africa, and international estimates in Kenya, Rwanda, Uganda and Zambia. Routine health information systems (404 records) were cited more than population estimates in South Africa and Uganda. National vital statistics were cited in 6 of 8 countries (291 citations, 31% of non-population evidence citations), absent from Uganda and Morocco. The Global Burden of Disease study was cited in 5 of 8 countries (South Africa and Kenya, 11 citations each; Rwanda, Uganda, and Zambia, 1 each), absent from Thailand, Morocco, and India. Only South Africa cited a domestically-produced burden-of-disease estimate distinct from the global study. A named local epidemiological model appeared in 3 of 8 countries, each for that country’s own HIV response (South Africa, Zambia, Uganda). Verbal autopsy was cited in 4 of 8 countries (1-7 citations each). A named local disease-surveillance system (e.g. Integrated Disease Surveillance and Response) was cited in 5 of 8 countries (107 citations). HDSS infrastructure was cited in only 1 of 8 countries (Kenya), despite verified HDSS research sites in several sampled countries.

Conclusion. Population estimates and census data dominate the evidence cited in documents that frame national health policy and budgets across all 8 countries, while global burden-of-disease estimates, domestic corrected-burden studies, verbal autopsy, and HDSS infrastructure are cited unevenly and, in most countries, rarely or not at all.

Routine health information systems are used more than vital statistics and health research less than vital statistics suggesting an evidence hierarchy in national health decision-making. Gaps and limitations of health research are potentially more well acknowledged, than vital statistics gaps may be under-recognized or unaccountaed for in policy decisions. The proposed hierarchy does suggests which evidence sources ought to be strenghtened in order to improve the overall evidence base for health policy. A useful next step is to explore the association between evidence used in policy and actual health spending. For example, to assess how far these cited evidence sources actually influence the apportioning of finite health budgets, and whether correcting known data-quality gaps may improve spending efficiency.

Keywords

Evidence-informed policy; health financing; civil registration and vital statistics (CRVS); burden of disease; comparative health systems; low- and middle-income countries; health information systems.
