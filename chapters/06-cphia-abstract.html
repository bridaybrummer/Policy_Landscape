Background. Whether health spending translates into better outcomes is itself a contested empirical question, already tested elsewhere in the South African literature. This project does not assume that spending should track disease burden in strict proportion — that is one plausible allocation heuristic among several, and reasonable people can disagree on how spending should be apportioned across need, cost-effectiveness, equity, or historical precedent. What is far less contestable is a narrower, prior claim: within a finite budget, some allocation decision is unavoidable, and whichever rule a government follows, that rule is only as good as the evidence used to inform it. Adjusted or corrected burden-of-disease estimates are not interchangeable with raw registered counts, which are susceptible to surveillance and reporting artefacts. South Africa’s civil registration and vital statistics (CRVS) system is a documented case of this risk: the SAMRC National Cause-of-Death Validation Project found only 36.9% agreement between officially registered and validated underlying cause of death across over 15,000 linked deaths, with a roughly four-to-one undercount of HIV/AIDS as underlying cause, substantial misclassification of injury deaths, and a high proportion of unusable codes. Two mechanisms plausibly drive these gaps: an unresolved tension between patient confidentiality and public health surveillance use of HIV-related death data, and non-specific (“ill-defined”) diagnoses written by certifying clinicians that central coders cannot reliably translate into ICD codes regardless of coder skill. If strategic planning documents anchor to registered (rather than corrected) burden estimates, whatever allocation rule they encode is being fed a distorted epidemiological signal — a risk concentrated in causes outside HIV/TB, including non-communicable diseases (NCDs) and violence/injury, which lack HIV/TB’s own dedicated correction model (Thembisa). This has not previously been empirically tested end-to-end against real provincial budget data.

Objectives. To build an open, reproducible pipeline that (1) assembles programme-level provincial health budget data, (2) traces which burden-of-disease data sources — both the global Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study and South Africa’s own SAMRC National Burden of Disease Study — are actually cited in the strategic planning documents that justify those budgets, and (3), in later work, quantifies the gap between registered and burden-corrected disease estimates as an input to a funding-gap analysis.

Methods. An AI-assisted extraction pipeline (R functions authored with AI coding-assistant support, run within an R {targets}-orchestrated pipeline) was built to fetch real, versioned provincial Health-department budget data (National Treasury’s Estimates of Provincial Revenue and Expenditure, “EPRE”) and to extract citations of known burden-of-disease data sources from National Department of Health strategic planning documents published over the last decade, using programmatic text search over the documents’ own published content. Citations were classified into three tracked evidence-source groups: registered mortality reporting (Stats SA’s own cause-of-death publications), corrected-burden estimates (the global GBD study and South Africa’s own SAMRC National Burden of Disease Study), and research/validation sources that could confirm or correct registered data’s accuracy (the SAMRC’s own National Cause-of-Death Validation study, Rapid Mortality Surveillance, and the Thembisa HIV model); citations of population-estimate/census sources were also identified but are not further dissected here. Citations of the health department’s own routine data/patient-monitoring systems (DHIS, TIER.net, EDRWeb) were tracked as a separate, fourth category throughout, so they never dilute the evidence-source counts above.

Preliminary results. A live pull of the 2024-25 EPRE release returned 42,578 real budget line items across all nine provinces (FY2020-FY2026) (see the Budget Panel chapter for the full interactive breakdown), confirming a consistent eight-programme provincial Health budget structure (see the Programme Crosswalk & Data Quality chapter for how raw programme-name variants were harmonised into that structure). A parallel extraction across six national strategic planning documents — three pre-2020 Annual Performance Plans (2017/18, 2018/19, 2019/20; the first of these covers the same fiscal year as the National Cause-of-Death Validation study’s own fieldwork) and three documents from 2020 onward (the 2020/21-2024/25 Strategic Plan, the 2025/26 Annual Performance Plan, and the 2025-2030 Strategic Plan) — found 122 citations of known burden-of-disease data sources (74 in the three pre-2020 documents, 48 in the three post-2020 documents), with Stats SA cited far more frequently (68/122) than either corrected-burden source: the Global Burden of Disease study (6/122) or the SAMRC’s own National Burden of Disease Study (5/122) — see the Citation Trail chapter for the full document-by-document, source-by-source breakdown behind every number in this paragraph. This is not a recent artefact: Stats SA’s share of citations is essentially unchanged across the pre-2020 and post-2020 document eras (52.7% vs. 60.4%; 39 of 74 pre-2020 citations vs. 29 of 48 post-2020). The two corrected-burden sources move in opposite directions over the same period: GBD’s share rose from 0% to 12.5% (0 of 74 pre-2020 citations vs. 6 of 48 post-2020), while the SAMRC National Burden of Disease Study’s share fell from 5.4% to 2.1% (4 of 74 vs. 1 of 48) — i.e. engagement with South Africa’s own domestically-produced corrected-burden estimate is shrinking even as engagement with a foreign corrected-burden estimate grows. Extending the citation trail to three provincial Annual Performance Plans (Gauteng, KwaZulu-Natal, Western Cape) — the sphere of government that actually sets EPRE allocations, not the National Department of Health — found these same sources cited less densely, and far more unevenly, than in national documents (39 citations in Gauteng’s APP vs. 14 and 13 in KwaZulu-Natal’s and Western Cape’s respectively), and found zero citations of the SAMRC National Burden of Disease Study in any of the three provincial documents. Reclassifying national citations into the three tracked groups above sharpens this picture further: registered-mortality citations are not being displaced by more corrected-burden or research/validation engagement over time — their own era share rose slightly (24.3% to 27.1%, pre- to post-2020 — 18 of 74 pre-2020 citations vs. 13 of 48 post-2020: a rising share of a shrinking pool) even as corrected-burden citations nearly tripled in share (5.4% to 14.6%; 4 of 74 vs. 7 of 48, GBD-driven), while research/validation citations — the ones that could actually justify trusting or correcting registered data — stayed essentially flat (8.1% to 8.3%; 6 of 74 vs. 4 of 48) and are carried almost entirely by Rapid Mortality Surveillance: the SAMRC’s own National Cause-of-Death Validation study, the source of this abstract’s own 36.9% validation estimate, has zero confirmed citations across all 9 documents sampled (see the Citation Trail chapter’s new registered-mortality-vs-corrected-burden-vs-research section for the full breakdown, including why this narrower “registered mortality” count is smaller than the raw “Stats SA” count above).

Conclusion. The decisions that translate disease burden into rand allocations are made mainly at provincial level, yet the planning documents that justify those decisions are anchored overwhelmingly in registered, uncorrected Stats SA statistics rather than either burden-corrected estimate — and provinces show even less consistent engagement with any of these sources than the National DoH does. A second, sharper finding sits inside that headline pattern: where corrected-burden evidence does appear, it is increasingly the global GBD estimate rather than South Africa’s own SAMRC National Burden of Disease Study, whose citation share is falling, not rising, and which is entirely absent from the three provincial documents sampled. For a conference organised around African health sovereignty, this is a concrete, quotable finding about what “ownership” of the evidence base for domestic health-financing decisions currently looks like in practice. Taken together with the two data-quality mechanisms above, this suggests two complementary, non-committal directions rather than a single fix: (1) giving domestically-produced corrected burden-of-disease estimates more visible standing in the documents that justify budgets, rather than defaulting to registered statistics or an externally-produced alternative, and (2) relocating ownership of diagnostic data quality to the certifying clinician who generates the diagnosis, since both confidentiality-driven under-recording and non-specific diagnoses originate at certification, not at central coding. A concrete next application of these data-quality deficits is a funding-gap analysis targeted at the causes shown above to be most exposed to registered-data distortion — non-communicable diseases (NCDs) and violence/injury-related spending — to investigate whether more efficient, proportional funding is possible once registered counts are replaced with corrected estimates. The more general, transferable point for other national and provincial policymakers is methodological rather than programmatic: before assuming that national or global burden-of-disease estimates are actually shaping local resource allocation, it is possible — and inexpensive — to check which sources are cited where the money is actually justified, using each jurisdiction’s own planning documents and data ecosystem rather than relying on any single external estimate. This case study demonstrates that check for South Africa; whether closing its own CRVS data-quality gaps needs investment precisely at the sphere of government shown here to be least engaged with burden-of-disease evidence is one of several questions it opens, for South Africa and potentially elsewhere.

Anticipated audience Q&A (prep notes, not part of the submitted abstract)

“Are these budgets funded by foreign aid or the local fiscus?” — likely given CPHIA’s health-financing-sovereignty framing. Every rand in this pipeline’s budget figures is domestic South African government expenditure (EPRE is a Treasury/provincial-legislature document, not a donor ledger); we have not yet split that into equitable-share vs. conditional-grant portions, and true foreign donor financing (PEPFAR, Global Fund) sits structurally outside this dataset. The ~10.6–10.8% domestic-only HIV/TB budget share (see the Descriptive Statistics chapter) is a floor, not the full picture — donor-vs-domestic decomposition is an explicit, not-yet-started next step (see the Plan & Open Questions chapter’s “Future / related projects” section), not something answered with unwarranted certainty here.
